# The game in more than one language

English and French ship today. The framework is `src/game/i18n.ts` (about 150
lines, no dependency), the dictionaries are `src/locales/*.json`, and two probes
stop the usual silent failures reaching a phone.

## Adding a string

1. Put the English in `src/locales/en.json`, under the namespace it belongs to.
   `en.json` is the source of truth: every other file answers to it.
2. Put the translation in every other file in that directory, at the same key.
3. Call it: `t('menu.newCareer')`, or `t('menu.continue', { manager, club })`.

That is the whole workflow. `npx vite-node scripts/i18nprobe.ts` fails if a key
is used and missing, missing from a translation, or carries different
placeholders in one language than another.

### Placeholders are named, never numbered

```json
"continue": "▸ Continue - {manager}, {club}"
```

A numbered or positional placeholder locks the sentence to English word order,
which is the single most common way a game gets localised badly. Named ones let
a translator move the pieces:

```json
"savedAt": "{season}, semaine {week}"
```

Numbers passed through `t()` are formatted for the locale on the way in, so a
French screen reads `12 000` rather than `12,000` - which a French player would
otherwise read as a decimal point.

### Plurals

An entry shaped `{ "one": ..., "other": ... }` is a plural, filled from `vars.n`:

```json
"injured": { "one": "{n} blessé", "other": "{n} blessés" }
```

English splits at `n === 1`. French keeps the singular for 0 as well, which
`t()` already knows; a language with more forms than two needs a rule added to
`t()` rather than a new key.

## Adding a language

1. Copy `en.json` to `src/locales/<code>.json` and translate the values.
2. Add the code to `Lang` and to `LANGS` in `src/game/i18n.ts`. The label goes in
   the language itself - "Français", not "French" - because somebody hunting for
   their language is scanning for the word they would use for it.
3. Add its number locale to `fill()` and its plural rule to `t()` if it needs
   one.
4. Run `scripts/i18nprobe.ts`. It will list every key you have not translated.
5. Run `node scripts/langprobe.mjs` after a build. It measures the bottom nav and
   the submenu at 412px, which is where a longer language shows up as a layout
   bug rather than a translation one.

## Where the choice lives

On the title screen, directly under the text size control, because it has to be
answerable before a career exists: a French speaker should not have to start one
in English to find the switch. Stored in `localStorage['rm-lang']`; with nothing
stored, the device's own language decides, but only between languages the game
actually has.

`store.lang` mirrors `i18n.getLang()` and `App` subscribes to it. That mirror is
load-bearing: `t()` is a plain function call, so without a store field nothing
would re-render when the dictionary under it changed, and the title screen would
stay English until something else happened to repaint it.

## The line: what follows the reader, and what keeps its language

**Data tables hold keys, screens call `t()`, and anything written into a save
calls `tIn('en', ...)`.** That one rule decides every case.

A screen is a view of NOW, so it speaks whichever language is switched on: every
label, button, heading, hint, tooltip and card - including the ones the engine
builds at render, like the assistant's game plan, the analyst's read, the
coach's verdict and its two fixes (`game/coachfix.ts`), the match billing
(`game/stakes.ts`), the trip note (`game/venue.ts`), the referee's notes
(`refNotes`), and the day room's treatment list. None of those are stored: they
are arithmetic on state, done again on every paint, so switching language
mid-career re-reads them all in the new one.

A save was a career's PAPERWORK, and paperwork kept the language it was written
in. **That rule was wrong about the reader, and it has been replaced.** It was
defensible in the abstract and indefensible in front of a player: switch the
game to French and every story in the inbox stayed English, for ever, because it
had been filed in English. A French player who has never once used English does
not have a career filed in another language; they have half a game they cannot
read. Reported from a live career on 24 August 2026, and correct.

### How a story is filed now

A story carries three things: the English `subject` and `body` exactly as
before, plus `k` (its key) and `v` (its variables). The screens render
`newsBody()` / `newsSubject()` from `model.ts`, which return `t(k, v)` when the
story has a key and fall back to the stored English when it does not.

**The stored English is not display text and must never be translated in place.**
The engine reads its own post back:

* `season.ts` pulls a transfer fee out of a body with a regular expression, and
  finds the international call-up story by its exact English subject in order to
  append the next window's names to it;
* `media.ts` looks for `joins {club}`, `insolvency.ts` for `cannot go on like
  this`, `gossip.ts` dedupes on `Community day at {short}`.

Translate those strings and call-ups, transfer follow-ups and insolvency stop
working - silently, in French only, months into a career. So they stay English
and the key renders alongside them.

The fallback is permanent, not a stopgap. Careers live on the device for years
and a save written today will be opened by a much later build; a story filed
before this existed, or assembled by appending to another, renders the English
it was filed in.

### Two conventions for the parts of a sentence that vary

`t()` substitutes, it does not recurse - so a fragment that needs translating
cannot simply be dropped into a variable as text.

* **`{thing_k}`** - a variable whose name ends in `_k` holds a KEY, looked up in
  the reader's language on the way in, one level deep. Without it,
  "expectations are {sky-high: silverware is demanded}" puts English back inside
  a French paragraph; with a key per combination instead, four stories become
  forty. A fragment may be a plural.
* **`{men_l}`** - a variable whose name ends in `_l` holds a JSON array of
  `{ k, ...vars }`, each rendered in the reader's language and joined on
  `common.listSep`. This is for a list where every item carries its own clause
  and its own plural - "Dupont (FL), rusty for 2 weeks; Martin (FH), rusty for 1
  week" - which cannot be hoisted out of the list into the sentence around it.
  Malformed JSON renders as the raw string rather than throwing, because it
  lives in a save file.

A story's subject key is its body key with `Subj` on the end. That is a
convention rather than a field, so `scripts/newsprobe.ts` checks that every body
has one - a missing subject renders a raw key as the headline, which is the most
visible way this breaks.

### The guard

`scripts/newsprobe.ts` is what makes this stick, because nothing about writing a
news story reminds you of any of the above - you are thinking about the story.
It fails the build when a story is filed with no key, when a `_k` or `_l`
fragment names a key that does not exist, when a story has no subject, or when
the two languages do not fill the same placeholders.

Its `BUDGET` is a ratchet over the sites not yet converted. **It may go down and
never up**, and the probe fails if it is left stale after a batch. At zero it
stops being a budget and becomes the rule.

### What still keeps its language

The match engine's own prose. Its commentary is pushed into the event stream as
it happens and the stream is the match report the save keeps - about 68,000
words of generated commentary, reports and season reviews. It is a translation
project rather than an extraction. Two consequences worth knowing:

* the pitch mock-up detects a scrum, a lineout or a maul by matching the
  commentary text, and those patterns keep working because the text they read is
  always English;
* a French match therefore has English commentary in the ticker with a French
  frame around it. That is a known gap, not a boundary anybody is defending.

Press questions and the answers you gave, a scout's note, a staff trait, a
stored personality and a grudge's reason are still stored English, and are
candidates for the same treatment as the news.

Dates are a screen thing: `weekDate`, `fixtureDate` and `dayDate` build from the
season and week the screen already holds, so nothing about a date is stored and
they read in the reader's language (`date.*` in the dictionaries).

## What is translated so far

Every screen. The title screen and the wizard, the nav and the submenus, the
squad, selection, tactics, training, transfers, finances, the club, the world,
the handbook, the day room and match day - the last of them being the biggest,
from the team sheet to the touchline panel to the full-time verdict.

The inbox follows the reader too, story by story as the conversion lands;
`newsprobe`'s budget is the honest count of what is left. What remains after
that is the match engine's commentary, above.

## The English side is a test selector

Forty browser harnesses find controls by their visible text - `text=New Career`,
`.submenu-item >> text=Tactics`. Rewording the English half of the dictionary
therefore reworks those tests, silently, into timeouts with no clue attached.
`i18nprobe` pins the handful they lean on hardest; the rest is a thing to keep in
mind before tidying English prose that reads fine.
