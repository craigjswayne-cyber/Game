# PHASE voice bible

How PHASE: Rugby Manager should sound, worked out from about 86,000 words of real
broadcast transcripts: six Premiership matches (including a play-off semi and a
final) and two autumn Tests, with commentary, co-commentary, studio punditry,
pitchside reports, referee and TMO audio, and live interviews with coaches and
players. The quotes below are short fragments, tidied of caption errors. Anything
marked **[extrapolated]** is not in the transcripts and is a best guess from how
British rugby media usually works.

Use this file whenever you write or rewrite player-facing copy: commentary, news,
inbox, board messages, scout and medical reports, tooltips and buttons.

---

## 1. What PHASE sounds like

**It does sound:** British. Rugby-native. Confident. Conversational. Specific.
Understated when that fits. Emotionally intelligent. Dry and funny now and then.
Concise. Varied. Human.

**It does not sound:** corporate, generic, Americanised, salesy, melodramatic,
repetitive, over-polished, falsely excited, robotic, or like an "AI sports
journalist".

In one line: **someone who knows the game, talking to someone else who knows the
game, without trying to impress them.**

---

## 2. Principles taken from the transcripts

**2.1 Short sentences. Fragments are fine.** Live speech is mostly 3 to 12 words,
and plenty of sentences have no verb. "Easy penalty." "Two from two." "Tackle on
top of the ball." "Big let-off for Saints." A pundit sums up a whole breakdown
plan as "You give Saints quick ball, you lose the game. Simples." If it would
work as a caption under a photo, it is about the right length.

**2.2 People say less than an AI would.** A coach asked what was going wrong at
the lineout said: "It's not one thing. It's not simple to sort out... line out
by line out." That's the whole answer. Nobody gives three reasons when one will
do. Nobody sums up their own answer at the end.

**2.3 Explain how, not how good.** Pundits rarely just say "brilliant". They
say what happened: "It's often because the carry is dominant. So the tackle ends
up on the wrong side." "There's an old saying, the kick is only as good as the
chase." The adjective comes second and the mechanism comes first. Game copy
should name the thing that happened (the chase, the clear-out, the exit, the
fold) instead of a vague quality like "intensity" or "execution".

**2.4 Praise the unseen work.** The warmest praise in the corpus goes to things
viewers miss: "Nine times out of 10, that's a complete unseen effort." "Don't
just focus on the tries. Every single kick he has chased." On exits: "You only
notice it when it goes wrong." A player who chased all afternoon is a better
story than the one who scored.

**2.5 Hedge, then commit.** "I think" turns up more than 200 times and "a little
bit" about 100, and that's from former internationals talking about their own
specialism. They hedge to sound reasonable, then land a firm view: "I think
that's a penalty try for me all day long." "I think it settles the argument."
Use one hedge per line at most, and never pile them up ("perhaps it may be
somewhat...").

**2.6 Tag questions do the agreeing.** "Isn't it?", "wasn't it?", "doesn't he?"
come up all the time (70-odd "isn't it" alone): "That's a great shot, isn't
it?" "He's rapid, isn't he?" It makes a judgement into a shared one. Use them in
commentary and punditry, sometimes in player chat, and never in system text.

**2.7 Criticism is direct, then given context.** (Pundits swear now and then; the game never does.) "What a penalty to give away.
Honestly, I'd be fuming." "School boy, it's what you used to do when you
were 16." The softer versions are still plain: "much easier said than done, but
Saracens will be disappointed with that." "He'll want that one again." "He'll
put that down to experience." "Not an excuse, it's a reason." Nobody writes
"there were areas for improvement".

**2.8 Understatement carries the big feelings.** A heavy hit is "a rib
inspection". A concussed player is "a real concern". A hammering is "a long old
way back". A fierce derby turns "a little bit spicy". Save loud words for loud
moments (see section 4).

**2.9 Humour is dry, quick and usually about people.** Some examples: the
running gag over whether a player is "James or Jimmy" ("we like to be formal
here"), "If you think your sport has the most bananas law book, hold our pints",
"the great big, very mobile wardrobe", a referee saying "I'm not trying to hand
them out like Pokémon". These are one-liners. Nobody explains the joke and
nobody follows it with "jokes aside".

**2.10 Familiar names, nicknames, team as a crowd.** Clubs get their short or
nick names (Saints, Tigers, Chiefs, Bears, Sarries). Players are "the 10", "a
back-rower", "the big Georgian", "the skipper", "his mates". Teams are plural:
"Bristol have", "England are". Write "Harrow have won four straight" and never
"Harrow has".

**2.11 Numbers are used, not recited.** "Saints have had 28 phases in the Bears
22 and just the one try", then the reason. Use one number per point, and only
when it proves something.

**2.12 Rugby people distrust their own clichés.** When a commentator called
something "the defining moment", the pundit replied "Multiple defining moments."
"At the risk of sounding like a broken record..." Self-aware is good. Earnest
clichés are not.

### Rugby vocabulary as it is actually used

| Term | What it means in use |
|---|---|
| breakdown / ruck | The contest after a tackle. "Speed into the ball in the turnover kills." |
| jackal / over the ball | A defender's hands on the ball after a tackle. Usually "over the ball", "got his hands on it", "a good jackal, he just doesn't roll away". |
| speed of ball / quick ball | How fast the ruck is recycled. "Every one of those rucks was 3 seconds or less." Slow ball means you can't play. |
| clear-out | Attackers removing jackallers. "Your first arriving player has to be brutal in his clear-out." |
| gain line / tackle line | Going forward past the point of contact. "Over the gain line", "behind the gain line". |
| win the collision | The carrier or tackler going forward on contact. A dominant tackle drives the carrier back. |
| carry / carrier | A run into contact. "Eighth carry for him." "A big carry." |
| exit | Clearing your own 22. "Another clean exit." "Their exits have been very good." |
| territory / field position | Where the game is played. "Kicking for field position and then putting pressure on." |
| kick pressure / chase | Hunting the kick. "A decent first chase." "The kick is only as good as the chase." |
| box kick / contestable | The scrum-half's high kick. Hang time, landing zone. |
| 50-22 | A kick from your own half that bounces out in their 22. Always worth a mention. |
| set piece | Scrum and lineout, plus the restart as "the third set piece". |
| maul / drive | Driving lineout. "Throw, catch and drive." "Go to the corner and back the maul." |
| red zone / 22 | Inside the opposition 22. "Five visits, four tries." |
| connection / disconnected | Defenders staying linked. "He's not connected... they will tear you apart." |
| fold / overfold | Defenders moving across after a ruck. A coach admitted "we've overfolded there." |
| line speed | How fast the defence comes up. "Their line speed got up and they forced that error." |
| accuracy | Finishing chances and executing skills under pressure. "The crucial thing for England is just that accuracy." |
| discipline | Penalty count, usually "in our 22". "We need to sort our discipline out." |
| game management | Closing out a match: kick, exit, keep the ball, take the points. |
| bench / bomb squad / impact | Replacements. "That's the role of the bench. Got to raise the energy." |
| bin / yellow / 20-minute red | Cards. "Off to the bin." "Ten minutes in the bin." |

## 3. Voices

### 3.1 Commentator (match lines)

Mostly names and verbs. Present tense. Rhythm comes from the play itself. The
co-commentator explains in short bursts, with a "Look at..." or "Watch how..."
on the replay.

- Do: names as sentences ("Kemp. Over the ball. Turnover."), plain verbs
  (carries, clears, hammers, scoots, pounces), the score said simply, one image
  at most per big moment.
- Don't: adjectives in every line, "incredible" on routine plays, explaining
  every pass, the same verb two lines running.

Example lines for the game:
- "Decent chase. Harrow have it back on their own 10."
- "Quick ball, and they're away down the short side. Ashworth. Still going. Try."
- "That's gone forwards. Scrum, Calder."
- "Big moment, this. Eight points in it and Whitby are camped on the line."
- "Good exit. You only notice those when they go wrong."

### 3.2 Coach (head coach or director of rugby, post-match and pitchside)

Says "we" and "the boys" or "the lads". Talks in process words: our breakdown,
our set piece, our discipline, the quality of the tackling, second efforts.
Mostly short, with an occasional "um", and doesn't care about polish. Credits
the opposition quickly ("fair play to them, good play"). Grievances are stated
as facts, not moaned about: "nine penalties in the 22 and still no yellow card."
On defeats, names one cause and the one fix, and then stops. Dry humour comes
out under pressure: asked whether a 150-kilo replacement was like having two
players on, "Well, two people does seem quite good."

- Do: plain diagnosis, credit, one fix, honest about injuries, protective of
  young players.
- Don't: "we showed great character", "a valuable learning experience",
  "the focus now turns to", speeches, quoting the whole stat sheet.

Example lines:
- "Really pleased with the tackling. Second efforts were there all afternoon."
- "We gave them too many entries into our 22 and they're too good to keep out
  for ever. That's on us."
- "Lineout cost us. Not one thing, a few things. We'll fix it."
- "He's fine, he's up and talking. We'll be careful with him."

### 3.3 Player (by experience and mood)

Players are shorter and more physical than coaches: "it's just physics
obviously", "you've got to be process-driven, but you've got to be violent as
well, otherwise they're just going to creep over." Lots of "obviously", "um",
"to be honest", "the boys".

- **Young or debutant:** nervous, thankful, keen to give the credit away. "Just
  tried to do my job. The boys made it easy." No grand statements about
  himself.
- **Established international:** calm, dry, a bit guarded. "We were all right.
  Plenty to tidy up."
- **Veteran:** relaxed, gently funny about age and the body. "Legs are telling
  me I'm 34. Head says go again."
- **After a heavy defeat:** fewer words, no cover. "Not good enough. Simple as."
- **After a big win:** a quick release, then back on the ground. "Massive for
  the club. Enjoy tonight, back in Monday."
- Don't: "I'm proud of the journey", "a dream come true" (unless he is 19 and on
  debut, and even then only once), more than two sentences in a row of
  reflection.

### 3.4 Journalist: Ruck (the news byline)

**[extrapolated, informed by the commentary register]** Ruck is a knowing,
slightly irreverent rugby outlet, not a wire service. Headlines are short, have
a verb, and sometimes a pun if a good one turns up naturally. Body copy uses
short paragraphs, with the main fact first and the context second. It's allowed
an opinion and a raised eyebrow. Quotes from coaches and players do the
emotional work.

- Do: put the fact first, use one sharp angle, use club nicknames, give exact
  numbers, have a dry last line about once in four stories.
- Don't: the "In a stunning turn of events" opening, a moral at the end, or
  "only time will tell".

Example lines:
- "Harrow sign Scottish lock on two-year deal"
- "Whitby's scrum creaked for an hour and then folded entirely. Their coach
  called it 'a bad day at the office'. It was a bad day at several offices."
- "Calder are three from three and nobody at the club is saying the word
  'title'. Everybody else is."
- "Rumour: Ashworth's 10 has had his head turned by a French offer. His agent
  says nothing has been signed. Nobody has mentioned whether anything has been
  discussed."

### 3.5 Board / Chairman

**[extrapolated from club statements and chairman interviews]** Formal but
human. Short sentences, and money talked about plainly. Warmth is rationed and
patience is spelled out. A British chairman writes "We expect to be in the top
half" and not "We are targeting a top-half finish going forward".

- Do: one clear expectation, one note of backing, a real deadline when there is
  one.
- Don't: buzzwords like "stakeholders", "synergy" or "vision", or overt threats.
  Pressure is in what's understated.

Example lines:
- "The board is happy with the start. We'd be happier with a win at home."
- "The budget is set at £4.2m. There is no more this season, and I'd rather you
  heard that from me now."
- "Two wins from ten is not where any of us wanted to be. You have our support.
  I'd like the results to make that easier to say."

### 3.6 Scout

**[extrapolated from co-commentary scouting talk]** Notes, not prose. Says what
he does, then the doubt. Uses the corpus's style of player description: "he's
all pointy shoulders and elbows", "deceptively big", "doesn't die in contact",
"great feet for a back-rower", "not the best lineout thrower".

Example lines:
- "Carries hard, doesn't die in contact. Lineout throwing is a worry."
- "Quick over 10 metres, slower over 40. Good feet. Needs a year in the gym."
- "21, plays like he's 28. Would start for most of the league."

### 3.7 Agent

**[extrapolated]** Polite, and always working an angle. Does the flattering and
the pressure in the same breath. Never says the number first.

Example lines:
- "He loves it here. I just think he deserves to feel valued."
- "There's interest from France. I'm telling you as a courtesy."
- "We're not asking for the world. We're asking for what he's worth."

### 3.8 Medical

Based on how the corpus talks about injuries: calm, specific, never dramatic.
Name the body part, give the timeline, then the caveat. Broadcasters soften a
bit ("a little bit of a niggle", "nursing a groin injury", "a knock"), and for
the serious ones they turn quiet and plain: "It's not just a little one. It
looks quite serious." "He went unconscious, so we have to treat this seriously."
Concussion is never a joke and never "just a knock".

Example lines:
- "Tight hamstring. Rest this week, should be fine for Saturday."
- "Failed his HIA. He'll follow the return-to-play protocol, so 12 days minimum."
- "Ruptured ACL. He's out for the season. We'll look after him."

### 3.9 System / UI

Plain, short, British, and with nothing written to sound like a person. Use
verbs on buttons ("Pick team", "Sign", "Release", "Continue") and sentence case.
No exclamation marks except where something has really been won.

- "Squad is over the cap by £120k."
- "3 players unavailable. Check medical."
- "Match day. Your team is picked."
- Not: "Oops! Looks like your squad needs some attention!"

## 4. Emotional temperature

The commentary works on a scale. Routine play gets names and plain verbs. A big
moment gets a short burst, sometimes shouted, and then the co-commentator calms
it straight down with an explanation. Even the final ends on plain facts: "Two
from two. Bristol 41, Northampton 26." The big flourishes ("a thunderous
play-off that will live long in the memory", "England open the history books")
come out maybe once a match. PHASE should be stingier than that.

| Moment | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|
| Win | Routine home win: "Job done. Five points, nobody hurt." | Final: "Champions. Calder have done it." Then one line of detail and stop. |
| Injury | Knock: "Took a bang on the shoulder, he'll be fine." | Serious: "Broken leg. Out for the season. Thoughts with him." No pun, no stats. |
| Defeat | Narrow: "Two points. One missed kick. That'll sting on the bus." | Hammering: "Seven tries conceded. Nothing to hide behind." |
| Milestone | Debut: "First start for the 19-year-old. Didn't look out of place." | 200th cap: "Two hundred. Most of them on a Saturday when nobody noticed the work. People notice today." |
| Season | Survival: "Safe. Not pretty, but safe." | Promotion: "Up. Ten years of trying and they're up." |

How the temperature goes up: sentences get shorter, more nouns, fewer
adjectives, names repeated. It does not go up by adding stacked superlatives.

## 5. AI fingerprints to hunt

These read as machine-made because they are abstract (no rugby noun in them),
balanced for the sake of balance, or tie things off like a school essay. None
of the following ever appears in the 86,000 words: "testament", "remains to be
seen", "the focus now turns", "journey", "positives", "proud".

| Kill on sight | Why it reads as machine-made | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| "There is plenty to..." | Empty opener. | Name the thing: "The lineout needs work." |
| "The challenge now is..." | It's a frame, not news. | "Next up: Harrow away." |
| "It will be important to..." | Says nothing a coach would say. | "We have to win the collisions." |
| "This represents a significant..." | Corporate. | "That's a big win." |
| "Despite X, Y" | A formulaic two-step. | Two sentences: "Lost the lineout. Won anyway." |
| "A disappointing result, but..." | The "but" cancels the feeling. | "Not good enough." Full stop. |
| "The team showed..." | Abstract, sounds like a report card. | Say what they did: "They tackled for 80 minutes." |
| "The club will be looking to..." | Guesswork filler. | Cut it, or quote someone. |
| "There are encouraging signs" | Vague. | Name the sign: "The scrum held for once." |
| "This is an opportunity to..." | Motivational poster. | "Win this and they're in the top four." |
| "They will need to demonstrate..." | Exam-board wording. | "They need a win." |
| "A positive step forward" | Corporate. | "Better." |
| "A key factor will be..." | Preview padding. | "Watch the breakdown." |
| "It remains to be seen" | A non-claim. | Make the call, or cut it. |
| "The focus now turns to..." | A link from a TV script. | "Next: Calder at home, Saturday." |
| "There is still work to be done" | Says nothing. | "Long way to go." Or name the work. |
| "The result highlights..." | Essay voice. | Say it straight. |
| "The performance demonstrated..." | Report voice. | Concrete detail. |
| "A valuable learning experience" | Nobody in rugby says it after losing. | "He'll put that down to experience." (used by a commentator, once, lightly) |
| "A difficult period, but..." | Hedged consolation. | "Rough month." |
| "The club continues to..." | Press-release voice. | Cut it. |
| "This could prove to be..." | Fortune-telling. | "Could be big." Or say nothing. |

**Structures to hunt as well:**
- **The dash hinge.** "Statement - wry coda." A tidy turn after a dash or colon
  at the end of a line. Use it rarely. Write two sentences, or drop the coda.
- **Aphorism closers.** "Sometimes the best defence is belief." Rugby people
  only do this when they're joking. End on a fact.
- **Tricolons.** "Grit, guts and determination." Lists of three on autopilot. The
  corpus has one deliberate one ("It is fast. It is furious. It is thrilling.")
  at the loudest moment of a play-off. Your copy is not that moment.
- **"Not just X but Y."** Generic emphasis. Pick X or Y.
- **Balanced paragraphs.** Positive, negative, positive. Real copy leans one way.
- **Summing up.** A last sentence that repeats the first one. Delete it.
- **Synonym cycling.** "the visitors... the away side... the travelling team" in
  three lines. Say "Harrow" again.
- **Stacked intensifiers.** "Truly remarkable, absolutely stunning." One word,
  or none.

**Genuine rugby stock phrases that real humans overuse too:** "defining moment",
"statement win", "turn the screw", "get out of jail free card", "on the front
foot", "big moment", "massive", "huge". They sound authentic, which is why
they're dangerous. Allow each one about once per screen, and never two in a
line.

## 6. British English and house rules

- **Spelling:** British throughout: defence, offence, centre, colour, favourite,
  organise, realise, programme, metres, practise (verb). (The transcripts spell
  "defense" because auto-captions are American. Ignore that.)
- **No em dashes anywhere.** Use a full stop, a comma, or a colon, or very
  occasionally a spaced hyphen ( - ). No en dashes in prose either.
- No emoji. Exclamation marks only for real triumphs, and no more than one.
- **Scores:** 24-18, home score first. In speech and commentary lines "nil" is
  fine ("five-nil up", "14-nil"). In tables and UI use 14-0. "Won by six",
  "lost by a point", "a seven-point game", "within one score".
- **Money:** pounds. £450k, £1.2m, "a £60,000 a year deal". The salary cap is
  "the cap".
- **Words:** match (the fixture), game (fine in speech and for the sport in
  general), pitch (not field, except "field position" and "field of play"),
  kick-off, half-time, full-time, the 22, the 10-metre line, try line, touch,
  squad (not roster), fixtures, the league table, top of the table, relegation,
  promotion, the Prem or the league, Test (capital T for internationals), caps.
- **Roles:** head coach, director of rugby (often "DoR" in news copy), attack
  coach, defence coach, scrum coach, forwards coach, head of S&C, chairman,
  chief executive. "Manager" means the player character only where the game's
  framing needs it. Inside the fiction, staff call you "coach" or "boss".
- **Positions:** loosehead, hooker, tighthead, lock or second row, blindside,
  openside, number eight (or "the 8"), scrum-half (the 9), fly-half (the 10),
  centre, wing, full-back, back-row, front-row, back three.
- **Plural teams:** "Harrow are", "the club have", "England were".
- **Numbers:** spell out one to nine in prose, digits for 10 and up. Always use
  digits for scores, minutes ("in the 63rd minute", "63 minutes in"), metres
  and money.
- **Time and dates:** Saturday 3pm kick-off. 7.45pm (the British full stop).

## 7. Calibration test

Read the line out loud before you ship it. Then ask:

1. **Would a real rugby person think a person wrote it?** A player, a coach, a
   season-ticket holder. If they'd wince, rewrite it.
2. **Does it sound SAID rather than WRITTEN to sound intelligent?** If you
   couldn't imagine it at a bar, on a sideline, or in a pitchside interview,
   it's too written.
3. **Does it sound like PHASE, or like generic AI sports-management copy?**
   Swap in another sport's words. If it still works, it's generic. Good PHASE
   copy has a rugby noun in it that couldn't belong anywhere else.

Quick checks: Is there a hedge piled on a hedge? A list of three? A line that
sums up? Any word from the section 5 list? An em dash? If so, fix it.
